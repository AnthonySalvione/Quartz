## 1. All-or-Nothing Thinking (Black-and-White Thinking)

Situations are seen in extremes rather than on a continuum.

*Example: You're on top of your coursework all semester. One assignment slips through
the cracks. Suddenly the entire course feels ruined — not worth engaging with
anymore. The one miss erases everything that came before it.

- E.g.: I have one thing (related to emotion) will cause me to feel that the whole
day is a failure.
- “Two things are true at the same time”

## 2. Mental Filter

Locking onto a single negative detail while the broader picture drops out.

*Example: You have a productive day — tasks done, obligations met. But one item on
your mental list didn't get checked off. That one gap becomes the whole story of the
day, and the rest of it stops counting.

E.g.: didn’t have a good morning revival, it doesn’t matter what happens after that, from
that point on, I’m trying to make up mistakes.

## 3. Catastrophizing (Fortune Telling)

Predicting that a negative outcome will spiral into something unrecoverable.

*Example: You feel nauseous in the morning. Immediately the thought follows: "I'm going
to vomit. The whole day is gone. I won't be able to do anything. Everything I had
planned is lost." The anticipation becomes its own catastrophe — often worse than
anything that actually happens.

## 4. Emotional Reasoning

Treating a feeling as though it's evidence that something is true.

*Example: "I feel like I've forgotten something important, so I must have forgotten
something." The feeling of incompleteness is real — but feeling like something is
missing is not the same as something actually being missing.

## 5. Possible Versus Probable

Confusing "this could happen" with "this is likely to happen."

*Example: You're about to eat something unfamiliar, or eat earlier in the day than usual.
The thought arrives: "What if this makes me sick?" And because that outcome is
possible, because it has happened before, it gets treated as likely enough to avoid. But
possible and probable are not the same thing. Most meals, including unfamiliar ones,
including morning ones, end without incident. The possibility of a bad outcome is real.
The probability is much lower than it feels.

## 6. Overgeneralization

Drawing a sweeping conclusion from a single event.

*Example: There was a period where the mental load became overwhelming and things
fell apart — obligations slipped, energy collapsed, functioning dropped. The conclusion
that followed: "This is what happens when I'm under pressure. I can't handle it." But that
period was one data point, under specific conditions, at a specific level of stress. It
doesn't define what's always true or what will always happen.

## 7. Thought-Action Fusion

Believing that having a thought makes it meaningful, dangerous, or predictive.

*Example: A distressing image surfaces — something related to your friend, or an
unwanted thought you didn't choose. The mind immediately asks: "Why did I think that?
What does it say about me?" But thoughts aren't actions, and they aren't character. An
unwanted thought appearing is no more meaningful than a song getting stuck in your
head.

## 8. "Should" and "Must" Statements

Holding rigid internal rules about how things are supposed to go, and experiencing
distress or shame when they don't.

*Example: "The service structure should be followed precisely. Everyone should
approach it with the same level of care I do." When others don't — when the system
isn't run the way it's supposed to be run — the distress isn't just frustration. It feels like
something has gone genuinely wrong.

## 9. Disqualifying / Discounting the Positive

Dismissing positive evidence because it doesn't fit the negative conclusion already in
place.

*Example: "I've managed to stay on top of most things today." "Sure, but the one thing I
missed means I can't trust myself." Strong performance across a dozen areas gets
cancelled out by a single gap — the positives don't register as real data.

## 10. Personalization

Assuming that your internal state or actions are the cause of outcomes that are outside
your control.

Example: A friend seems distant or off. The immediate conclusion: "I must have done
something. I've let something slip in how I've been showing up for them." No evidence,
no conversation — just the assumption that the explanation is you.

## 11. Jumping to Conclusions

Reaching a firm interpretation before enough information is available.

Example: An object needs to be thrown away — a receipt, a small keepsake. The mind
immediately concludes: "If I get rid of this, I'll lose the memory attached to it. It'll be
gone." The conclusion arrives before there's any evidence that the memory would
actually disappear.

## 12. Magnification / Minimization

Inflating the significance of what went wrong while shrinking what went right.

Example: "The fact that I couldn't get out of bed during that low period proves
something is fundamentally broken in me. The weeks I functioned well and held
everything together — that was just running on fumes. It doesn't count."

## 13. Tunnel Vision

Narrowing attention exclusively onto one dimension of a situation while everything else
disappears.

Example: There's a low mood period coming — you can feel it. Everything else that's
true right now — the things that went well, the relationships that are intact, the work that
got done — narrows down to a single focal point: the low that's coming, and the
certainty that it will be bad.

## 14. Labeling

Assigning a fixed, global identity based on an isolated experience.

Example: "I couldn't follow through on what I planned for recess. I never actually
recover the way I intend to. I'm someone who just doesn't execute." One pattern
becomes a permanent trait, and the trait becomes the whole story.

## 15. Mind Reading

Assuming you know what others are thinking without direct evidence.

Example: You present something — at work, in a meeting, in a service. Someone's
expression shifts or they seem disengaged. "They think I don't know what I'm doing." No
one has said anything. But the interpretation is already certain.
